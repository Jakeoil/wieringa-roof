Every piece below is cut from the same golden rhombus. Solid lines are cuts, dashed lines are creases — dash length gives the fold angle, colour gives mountain or valley. Sheets are drawn at true size, so Print → Save as PDF gives you 1:1.

Folding

Score every dashed line and fold it to its marked angle — 36°, 72° or 108°, and nothing else. Flip M/V prints the dual roof — the same surface reflected vertically, hills for dales — which exchanges every mountain and valley while leaving the cut shapes and fold angles identical. There are no flat joins on this surface, so every interior line really is a crease. Red is a ridge (mountain), blue a trough (valley); turning a whole piece over swaps them, so if a piece is easier to fold the other way up, that is fine.

Methods

Branch cuts does not grow a region at all. Counted over every patch, Eint = Vint + F − 1 exactly, which means a one-piece net cuts precisely one edge per interior vertex. Contract the whole boundary to a single point and such a cut set is a spanning tree of the vertex graph — its branches running from interior vertices out to the edge of the patch, exactly the branch cuts of log and √ that make a multivalued thing single-valued. Connectivity is then guaranteed rather than hoped for, and the only thing left to search for is a routing that does not overlap. Every patch here through generation 3 comes out as a single overlap-free sheet. The default.

Widened ribbons takes the longest de Bruijn ribbon as a backbone and then accretes neighbouring rhombi onto it. It gathers the most into one piece — 80–90% of the patch — leaving the rest as small slivers, so there are fewer substantial joins than the alternatives.

BFS unfolding spreads outward in rings from a single seed rhomb. Fewer pieces in total, but they are more evenly sized, which means more real nets to fold and join.

Where a patch comes apart into more than one piece it is because the surface has saddle vertices — more than 360° of paper wants to meet at a point, so the faces around it cannot all stay joined. Join those pieces along their matching cut edges when assembling. See Mathematics for why.
