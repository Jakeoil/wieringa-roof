Solid nets

The triacontahedron and its twenty golden hexahedra, flattened for paper. Cut the twenty out in the Kowalewski colouring and you have the pieces of the puzzle itself — not a picture of it.

How it works

Every solid here unfolds whole. The triacontahedron's thirty faces come apart into a single connected net with no overlap, and so does each of the twenty hexahedra — checked, along with the things that would ruin a model without being visible on screen: every edge keeps its length and every corner its angle, to within 1e-9. The angles are 63.43° and 116.57° throughout, because every face of every solid on this page is the same golden rhombus.

Solid lines cut, dashed lines fold. An edge shared by two faces of the net is a fold; every other edge is where the scissors go. There are no glue tabs — this is a cut-and-tape net, the same convention the roof Sheets use.

Kowalewski five. The colouring is a proper edge colouring of K6 on the six icosahedral axes: a face is spanned by two of them, and faces sharing an axis must differ. Three things follow rather than being imposed — every rosette of the triacontahedron shows all five colours; every hexahedron's opposite faces agree, since its six faces are three opposite pairs, one per axis pair of its triple; and each hexahedron therefore wears exactly three of the five. There are C(5,3) = 10 such triples, and each is borne once by an acute cell and once by an obtuse one — which is precisely the twenty labelled pieces of the classical Kowalewski puzzle. George Hart counts 320 colour-matching ways to put them back into the shell.

See the Cage for the dissection these come from, and the Workbench for the same five colours on the roof itself — where, unexpectedly, they also come out a proper colouring of the surface.

Size. The side is the rhombus edge, never a diagonal. At a 1 inch side the diagonals are 1.7013 in and 1.0515 in, and the whole triacontahedron net is about 7.8 by 8.9 side lengths — too big for one Letter sheet at that size, so drop to around 12 mm if it must fit on one page. The twenty hexahedra are small enough to print together at any sensible size.
