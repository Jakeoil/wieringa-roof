Intersection

In the packing, triacontahedra overlap. This asks what they actually share. Slide the second one out from coincident to the offset and watch the shared body appear between them — only one of the four offsets leaves anything with a name.

How it works

Where the offsets come from. On the Centers page every rhomb of the roof lies on two triacontahedra, and those solids sit at a small number of separations from one another. Four of them are worth looking at, and they are the four here. Everything below was computed by exact vertex enumeration over the thirty bounding planes rather than by sampling, so the volumes are good to the last digit shown.

One axis — separation 1.0000. The shared body is the rhombic icosahedron: 6.155367, which is exactly half the triacontahedron's volume, on 22 vertices and 20 faces every one of them a parallelogram. This one has a reason behind it. A triacontahedron is the zonohedron on six axes, and translating a zonohedron by one of its own generators and intersecting deletes that generator — what is left is the zonohedron on the other five, which is the rhombic icosahedron by definition.

Short diagonal — 1.0515, the golden rhombus's short diagonal. Shares 5.836483, on 30 vertices and 28 faces. All four-sided, but only 20 of them are parallelograms, so it is not a zonohedron.

Long diagonal — 1.7013. Shares 2.655892 on 16 vertices, and eight of its eighteen faces are triangles. A zonohedron has no triangular face at all.

Face contact — 2ρ = 2.7528. Nothing. The two solids meet on a single face and share no volume, which is the kissing distance of the Packing page.

Why only the first has a tidy answer. A three-dimensional polytope is a zonotope exactly when every face is centrally symmetric, and only the one-axis body manages it. That is also why only its volume has a closed form: neither diagonal volume can be written as (a + b√5)/c for small integers, where every zonohedral volume in this chapter can. A diagonal is not a generator, so the deletion argument has nothing to say about it.

Colour says which parent cuts. Each face of the shared body lies on a bounding plane of one solid or the other — blue for the first, amber for the second, green where the two coincide. The split is always even, as it must be: the shared region is centrally symmetric about the midpoint of the two centres.

And none of them is a union of whole cells. That was the hope — if the overlaps were unions of the twenty golden hexahedra of the Cage, then the packing and the dissection would be the same combinatorics twice. They are not, for any offset and for either dissection. Even the good case fails: the one-axis body is the rhombic icosahedron, but translated by half an axis, so it sits half a cell out of step and cuts ten of the twenty. The packing and the dissection stand on the same six axes and are offset from one another by a half-lattice step.
