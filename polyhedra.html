Polyhedra

Three solids underlie the whole construction, and every face of all three is the same golden rhombus. Drag any of them to turn it. They are built from the real geometry — the triacontahedron as a zonohedron on the six icosahedral five-fold axes, so its faces come out golden rather than being drawn that way — and they carry the same colours and isogloss contours as the roof itself, so the family resemblance can do the explaining.

Drag to turn it. Stood on a five-fold axis: five blue at each pole, five yellow in each temperate band, ten orange round the equator. Each blue cap is a Pe5 rosette in all but name. The contours are level sets of height, sliced pole to pole, so they close into rings of latitude the way the roof's run on from tile to tile.

The triacontahedron

Thirty golden rhombi close up into a solid, and every one of its dihedral angles is the same: 144°. Its 32 vertices are the 12 of an icosahedron together with the 20 of a dodecahedron, which is why it has two kinds of corner:

CornerCountFaces meetingAngular defect
Acute (icosahedral)125+42.825°
Obtuse (dodecahedral)203+10.305°

Descartes demands the defects sum to 720° for any convex solid, and they do: 12(42.825°) + 20(10.305°) = 720° exactly. Its volume for edge a is 4√(5+2√5) · a³ ≈ 12.31073 a³.

The two rhombohedra

Squash a cube until all six faces are golden rhombi and there are exactly two ways to do it — depending on whether the three edges at the special corner meet at the rhombus's acute or obtuse angle. These are the pieces the roof surface actually sits on.

Acute (prolate)

Three 63.4349° corners at the apex. Every dihedral angle 72°.

Blue at the apexes, yellow round the waist.

Obtuse (oblate)

Three 116.5651° corners at the apex. Every dihedral angle 144°.

Each has a single dihedral angle throughout, which makes them unusually easy to build. And their volumes stand in the ratio 0.76085 / 0.47023 = 1.618034 — φ, inevitably.

Ten of each make a triacontahedron. 10(0.76085 + 0.47023) = 12.31073 = 4√(5+2√5), the triacontahedron's volume exactly. The same two rhombohedra also fill space aperiodically — they are the three-dimensional analogue of the thick and thin Penrose rhombi, and it is the layer of them beneath the surface that gives the Wieringa roof its shape.
