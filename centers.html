Centers

Follow each face's normal and see where they meet. A point where several concur is the center of a rhombic triacontahedron, and the faces that named it are a piece of that solid's surface. The roof stops being a surface and becomes a lid.

How it works

Why a normal finds a center at all. The rhombic triacontahedron is isohedral — its symmetry group is transitive on its thirty faces — so all thirty face planes are tangent to one insphere, and because each face has a two-fold axis through its own middle, each touches that sphere at its centroid rather than anywhere else. A golden rhombus that is a face of a triacontahedron therefore fixes the solid's center to one of exactly two points, centroid ± ρ·n̂, with ρ = √(1 + 2/√5) = 1.3763819. One above the map, one below.

This works for the triacontahedron and for nothing else in the family. Built on the same axes but with fewer of them, the rhombic icosahedron, the Bilinski dodecahedron and the two golden rhombohedra are none of them isohedral: their face planes sit at two or three different distances from the center, and the offset is not even parallel to the face normal. So a concurrence at ρ identifies a triacontahedron specifically.

Ten is the ceiling. The roof lifts on five of the six icosahedral five-fold axes; the sixth is vertical. A solid's thirty faces therefore split into ten the roof can carry pointing up, ten pointing down, and ten that use the vertical generator — and those ten stand exactly vertical, which a surface with one height over each point of the plane can never contain. Of the ten it can carry, the five thick rhombi are the solid's top cap, tilted 31.7175° from horizontal, and the five thin ones are the ring below them at 58.2825°. Every solid on this page is a translate of every other: the frame is fixed, so nothing is ever rotated.

A complete cap is a Pe5 pentagon. This is the thing worth looking at. Wherever all ten faces are present, the five thick ones turn out to be exactly the five rhombi of one Pe5 tile of the P1 layer, and the ring of five thin ones comes from its neighbors. The count of complete solids equals the count of Pe5 rosettes in every patch tested — nine seeds, generations 2 to 4, up to 16,475 rhombi, without exception. The rosette's hub is the solid's own five-fold pole, exactly φ = 1.618 side lengths from the center, straight up or straight down.

Whether the solid sits below the roof or above it follows from the hub: a hub at the top of the patch's index range puts the solid underneath, a lid over something; a hub at the bottom puts it above, the roof forming its floor. The readout counts both.

The complete ones never touch. No two are closer than φ³ = 4.236 side lengths, where mere face contact would need 2ρ = 2.753 — they are isolated grains. The partial solids are another matter entirely: they interpenetrate freely, with centers as close as 1.7013, the golden rhombus's own long diagonal. Only the complete ones are drawn here, because drawing the rest fills the view with overlapping shells.

Vertical scale is ±1 and nothing between. The 3D page will flatten the roof continuously, which is honest there — scaling the vertical is an affine map, so faces stay planar. It would not be honest here. A squashed triacontahedron is not a triacontahedron, and its normals stop meeting at a point, which is this page's entire claim. So dales up is a flip and not a slider, applied by reflecting the whole scene, solids included.

The normals are the argument; everything else is its conclusion. Turn them on and pull the length out. Each face sends a segment both ways, colored by the solid it is heading for, and at exactly ρ = 1.3764 — where the slider detents — every one of them lands on a center, on its own marker, in its own color. Ten arriving together is a complete triacontahedron. Push past ρ and they carry on through and out the other side: normals do meet at other distances, at ρ/φ³, ρ√5, 3ρ and ρφ³ among others, but never more than a handful at once. Only ρ builds anything, and watching the rest fail is worth more than being told.

Solid size shrinks each triacontahedron toward its own center. This is safe in a way the vertical scale is not: a shrunk triacontahedron is still a triacontahedron, its faces stay parallel to the roof faces they came from, and its normals still meet at exactly the same point — so no setting of this slider can show you something false. It also fixes a rendering problem rather than only a crowding one, since the shells are translucent and where they overlap the blending is order-dependent. At zero a solid is its center, which is what the markers already draw. It detents at φ⁻¹, φ⁻² and φ⁻³ — φ³ being the inflation factor of the tiling in three dimensions, so a solid shrunk that far is the next one down in the hierarchy.

Colors mean grouping and nothing else. A face lies on two solids and no part of the geometry prefers either, so any single-valued coloring is a choice — here, largest group first. Complete caps take their color at strength and partial groups are washed toward gray, so the whole caps stand out of the field without needing a legend.
