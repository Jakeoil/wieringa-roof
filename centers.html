Centers

Follow each face's normal and see where they meet. A point where several concur is the center of a rhombic triacontahedron, and the faces that named it are a piece of that solid's surface. The roof stops being a surface and becomes a lid.

How it works

Why a normal finds a center at all. The rhombic triacontahedron is isohedral — its symmetry group is transitive on its thirty faces — so all thirty face planes are tangent to one insphere, and because each face has a two-fold axis through its own middle, each touches that sphere at its centroid rather than anywhere else. A golden rhombus that is a face of a triacontahedron therefore fixes the solid's center to one of exactly two points, centroid ± ρ·n̂, with ρ = √(1 + 2/√5) = 1.3763819. One above the map, one below. This works for the triacontahedron and for nothing else in the family: the rhombic icosahedron, the Bilinski dodecahedron and the two golden rhombohedra are none of them isohedral, so their face planes sit at two or three different distances from the center and the offset is not even parallel to the face normal.

Ten is the ceiling. The roof lifts on five of the six icosahedral five-fold axes; the sixth is vertical. A solid's thirty faces therefore split into ten the roof can carry pointing up, ten pointing down, and ten that use the vertical generator — and those stand exactly vertical, which a surface with one height over each point of the plane can never contain. Of the ten it can carry, the five thick rhombi are the top cap, tilted 31.7175° from horizontal, and the five thin ones the ring below them at 58.2825°. Every solid here is a translate of every other: the frame is fixed, so nothing is ever rotated.

Proper rhombs, and the four classes

A rhomb wears one nail. The head lies flat on its far face, the shaft runs the other way, ρ long, and it ends exactly on the center of the triacontahedron that rhomb belongs to. One rhomb, one solid, one direction — that is the picture, and for nine rhombi in ten it is the whole of it.

Strictly the normal is a line, and it carries a candidate at ρ on each side; both are centers of triacontahedra having the rhomb as a face, which is why the far side has anything for a nail head to sit on. What settles the direction is that only one of them is normally a proper class. On Sun at generation 4, 90.5% of rhombi have exactly one proper solid. 3.9% have none — those are the demoted ones. And 5.6% really do have two, always the same pairing: a class 4 on one side against a class 5b on the other. Those are the only rhombi that genuinely belong to two triacontahedra, and the one they are drawn with is the larger.

They have their own color on this page, and they need one. Home is the larger of the two solids and 5 beats 4, so left alone every shared rhomb takes the class 5b violet — which means every class 4 renders as only two amber faces, the two opposite its gap, with its other two handed to a neighbor. A class 4 drawn in its own color is always half a class 4. The shared color says which rhombi those are.

They sit at named positions, if you want to find them. Every one is thick — no thin rhomb is ever shared. In a class 4, whose four thick faces have a fifth missing, the shared pair are the two flanking that gap, never the two opposite it; every settled class 4 has exactly two, one at each end of its gap, without exception. In a class 5b, whose faces run T t T t T, the shared one is always an end of the run — never the middle thick, never either thin. The two classes are not symmetric in this: a class 4 always reaches into two 5b runs, while about half of all 5b runs end against nothing.

A proper rhomb, then, is one whose solid shows the roof a whole configuration rather than a scrap, and there are exactly four such configurations. They are told apart by makeup and not by size: class 5 comes in two quite different shapes, and lumping them would hide that.

The ten faces a solid can show a roof project, in plan, to a decagon — five thick round the pole in a Pe5 rosette, five thin filling the notches, tiling it exactly. So a class is a picture, drawn here the way the roof is drawn everywhere else, with the isogloss contours dividing each long diagonal into eight and the height ramp running dark at the low corner to light at the high one. Absent faces are dashed.

The rose rings mark the shared faces — the rhombi that lie on two proper solids at once. In the class 4 they are solid, because both faces flanking its gap are always shared; in the class 5b they are dashed, because either end of the run may be shared and about half of all runs share neither. A ringed face in one diagram is the same rhomb as a ringed face in the other, seen from the two solids it belongs to — which sit on opposite sides of the roof, centers exactly 2ρ apart, touching along it.

The four colored ones are the classes; the five gray ones are demoted, and they are demoted for reasons rather than by taste. Class 3 is never anything's home at all — every three-face solid is the far end of some other rhomb's normal. Classes 1 and 2 fall away as the patch grows: on Sun they run 17.9%, 2.7%, 0.4%, 0.05% at generations 2 to 5. They are boundary residue. Demoted rhombs stay drawn, shaded and contoured, but colorless.

Only these nine makeups occur, at any patch or generation measured up to 111,925 rhombi. There is no 1T+1t, no 2T+1t, no 3T+1t: thick and thin mix only at 5b and at the complete ten. Everything else is pure.

Class 10 is a Pe5 pentagon. Wherever all ten faces are present the five thick ones are exactly the rhombi of one Pe5 tile of the P1 layer, and the count of complete solids equals the count of Pe5 rosettes in every patch tested. The rosette's hub is the solid's own five-fold pole, exactly φ = 1.618 side lengths from the center, straight up or straight down. That also pins its frequency exactly: the substitution over the six P1 types has Perron eigenvalue φ⁴, giving Pe5 = φ⁻³/√5 and so φ⁻⁵/√5 = 0.0403252 complete solids per rhomb.

Nail heads

Half of what the construction emits is the far end of something else. A solid no face calls home is a nail head, and it is not a class: counting it counts the same rhomb twice. A complete cap generates ten of them; 99.9% of one-face solids are nail heads; they outnumber the real solids about four to one. They are held back everywhere on this page.

With normals on, a rhomb sending a normal one way shows a small white disk lying on its other face — a tenth of a unit across, single-sided, so it is visible only from the side with no normal. From that side you see a head; go round and you see the nail.

A solid is also held back when its ten candidate faces do not all lie inside the patch, because then it may be short only from the cut. Holes are a different matter: this generator draws rhombi from the P1 pentagons alone, so every star, boat and diamond leaves a gap at a density that does not fall with generation, and a solid truncated by a gap is honestly truncated.

The controls

Global, and the z-orientation. Patch and generation, then the orientation block. Heads is the default and it is definable rather than merely chosen: every generator has z = +1/√5, so a vertex sits at exactly index · s/√5 and heads is the orientation in which the index runs upward. Tails is its reflection — a second, equally valid Wieringa roof over the same tiling, with every hill a dale. Nothing about the surface itself tells them apart, since it has hills and dales either way; the anchor is the index, the one part of all this that carries no semantics of its own.

Flat collapses the roof into its own shadow — and that is not a metaphor, it is the Penrose tiling this whole site is a lift of. Switching parity runs down to flat, turns the roof over, and runs back out. During the motion the roof travels alone, because the solids would visibly spin a tenth of a turn at the switch: mirroring a triacontahedron is a 36° rotation, not the identity. Asking for the other parity while flat is asking to see it, so that turns flat off and rises straight into it. Stopped at flat they come back, and they are worth seeing there — the solids and normals stand at their true heights while the surface lies flat beneath them, which is the structure with its roof taken away. The rhombi keep their colors throughout; what a rhomb is does not depend on how deep the roof is.

Heads solids sit above the roof in the default orientation, tail solids below it. The distinction is intrinsic and does not move: turn the roof over and you see the same solids from underneath, rather than being handed the other set. Beware the hub, which runs the other way — a solid below the roof has its rosette hub at the top index, 396 of 396 measured — so this cannot be defined by where the hub sits.

Rhombs. Surface solid, transparent or invisible — invisible leaves the creases and contours standing, which is how you see the solids underneath. Note that transparency needs something to sit against: a shell at low opacity over the drawn surface reads as colored, and the same shell over the bare page washes out almost to white. The solids compensate, drawing heavier when the surface is hidden, but Solids: solid is the honest setting for looking at shells on their own. Shading follows parity and reads height on an absolute ramp across the whole patch; isoglosses are level sets of height, continuous from tile to tile. Both are clues to orientation rather than decoration.

Normals and triacontahedra have global settings, and each class overrides them: the globals are master switches that write through to all four, and the per-class boxes are the state from then on. Length detents at ρ, where every normal lands on its center, and runs past it on purpose — normals do meet at ρ/φ³, ρ√5, 3ρ and ρφ³, and none of those gathers more than a handful of faces. Only ρ builds anything, and watching the rest fail is worth more than being told. Cup only draws the ten faces the roof can touch instead of all thirty, which is all of a solid the roof ever sees. Inscribed sphere draws the insphere instead of the polyhedron — the ping-pong ball. Its radius is not fitted: the triacontahedron is isohedral, so all thirty face planes are tangent to one sphere of radius ρ, each touching at its own face's centroid. So the ball touches the roof at the exact middle of every rhomb that solid carries, and nowhere else — verified to 4e-15, with the nearest rhomb corner standing 0.097 clear of the surface. It is the tangency the whole page rests on, made literal. Being its own mirror image, the sphere also needs no parity handling, so it is drawn from the center alone.

The balls show the two populations apart at a glance. Complete solids never touch: 0 intersecting pairs of 224,785 on Sun at generation 4, no two centers closer than φ³ = 4.236 against the 2ρ = 2.753 that contact would need — a scatter of isolated grains. Turn the other classes on and the picture changes completely: 4,645 intersecting pairs, with centers as close as 1.7013, the golden rhombus's own long diagonal. Those are the intersecting ping-pong balls. Size shrinks each solid toward its own center: safe, unlike the vertical scale, because a shrunk triacontahedron is still one and its normals still meet at the same point — and it fixes a rendering fault as well as crowding, since the shells are translucent and where they overlap the blending is order-dependent.

Per class — show, normals, solids, and how big to draw them, with a live population count.

Generation 5 is available, and on the two big composites it is genuinely big: the Sun is 111,925 rhombi and about two seconds to generate, its triacontahedra included. That happens once per patch and is then cached, so the controls stay responsive afterwards — but the solids themselves will not all draw. 19,056 proper solids at the full thirty faces is 3.4 million vertices rebuilt on every change, so past a budget the page says so instead of hanging. Inscribed sphere has no such limit — it is one instanced mesh however many there are, which is what makes it the right extent at this size.

Daylight between the cups. With the surface invisible and every class's cups at full size you might expect the roof to be entirely covered, and it is not. That is not a gap in the geometry — a cup does contain every roof rhomb on its own solid, always — but a rhomb is covered only if its home solid is being drawn, and two kinds are held back by default. Truncated solids run off the edge of the patch, so their count is a lower bound rather than a class; they account for most of the daylight, and nearly all of it is the outer fringe. Demoted solids are the class 1 and 2 homes, few but real in the interior: Sun at generation 4 has ten of the first — the true orphans, and that ten does not grow with the patch — and fifty of the second. Turn both on and the coverage is total, necessarily: every rhomb has a home, and a home's cup contains it. The readout counts what is left bare.

Click any rhomb to see both of its solids, which is its home, and whether either is complete — drawn whatever the filters are hiding. Two rhombi pin a solid uniquely, so clicking a neighbor is the quick way to test the rule that two faces share a solid exactly when the fold between them is 36°.
