The three Penrose tilings

Tiles
P1pentagon, star, boat, diamondthe original, 1974
P2kite, dart
P3thick rhomb, thin rhomb

A mnemonic, since the numbering is easy to get backwards: Pentagons, Pointy, Parallelograms — 1, 2, 3. Tile-count will not help you, since P2 and P3 both have exactly two; only kite-and-dart versus rhombs will.

P1's pentagons are often counted as three separate prototiles, differently marked to carry the matching rules, so you will also see "P1 has six tiles". Its diamond is the thin rhomb, 36°/144°. All three sets are mutually locally derivable — the same tilings in different clothing, with the same φ inflation. This site uses P1 to generate and P3 to build: the seeds are P1 clusters, the paper is golden rhombi.

The P1 hierarchy

The prettiest fact in the whole construction is that the P1 tiles map onto small groups of rhombs, and the entire tiling can be built from the rhomb versions of the pentagons — and nothing else:

The three Pe tiles are pentagons. What their rhombs make at generation 1 merely resembles a star, a boat and a diamond — which is where the cluster colors got their names — but the tiles themselves are the marked pentagons of P1, and St5, St3, St1 are the real star, boat and diamond. The star-type tiles emit no rhombs at all at generation 1; they first contribute a generation later. Proper names are used throughout, appearances notwithstanding.

And for one of the three the hierarchy closes on itself exactly. Pe5, the pentagon cluster, has a convex hull that is a regular pentagon at every generation — not converging to one, already one. Comparing the normalized support function of the hull from generation 1 to generation 5 gives differences of order 10−16, the hull is five-fold symmetric to the same precision, and its support-function ratio is 1.236068 = 1/cos 36° = 2/φ at every generation, which is the signature of a regular pentagon and nothing else. A pentagon expands into a pentagon, at every scale, on the nose.

Pe3 and Pe1 behave more modestly. Their outlines do settle — successive generations differ by 0.095, 0.074, 0.047, 0.017 and 0.157, 0.112, 0.068, 0.026 respectively, each step closing faster than the last — so each converges to a definite limiting shape. But that limit is not the seed they grew from: the distance back to generation 1 plateaus around 0.16 and 0.21 rather than falling away. A substitution converges to its own attractor, and there is no reason in general for the attractor to be the tile you started with. For the pentagon it is; for the other two it is a near miss.

Of the uncountably many Penrose tilings, exactly two have exact five-fold symmetry — the ones seeded by Pe5 and St5, traditionally the sun and the star. They stand opposite each other: everywhere one has a five-fold rosette of tiles the other has a five-fold gap, and vice versa.

There is also a composite seed worth knowing: the decagon. Its figure is a yellow pentagon with two diamonds, two orange pentagons and a boat packed around it — six pieces — but the first rhombs it produces are only a boat and two diamonds. Star-type pieces emit nothing at generation one, so the whole of the decagon's first layer comes from the pentagons alone:

(3 thick + 1 thin) + 2 × (1 thick + 2 thin) = 5 thick + 5 thin = 10 rhombs

Which is exactly the dissection of a regular decagon into rhombs — ten of them, one for each pair of the five edge directions, five wide and five narrow.

It is also the one seed of the seven with no five-fold symmetry. Pe5 and St5 are invariant under every 72° turn; the decagon is invariant under none of them. What it has instead is a single mirror axis — verified at generations 2, 3 and 4 — so its left and right halves reflect each other while up and down do not. It is the one seed that grows a patch with a preferred direction.

The golden rhombus

One shape does all the work. A golden rhombus is the rhombus whose diagonals stand in the ratio φ : 1 — so the rectangle you get by drawing both diagonals is a golden rectangle. Its acute angle is

θ = arctan 2 = arccos(1/√5) = 63.4349°,   and 116.5651° obtuse.

Why one shape suffices

Take the six five-fold axes of an icosahedron. Put one on the vertical and the other five form a cone around it:

Ej = ( 2/√5 · cos 72j° ,  2/√5 · sin 72j° ,  1/√5 ),   j = 0…4

A Penrose vertex Σ nj ζj in the plane lifts to Σ nj Ej in space. Because Ej · Ek = ±1/√5 for every pair, all ten face orientations are the same golden rhombus. The thick and thin Penrose rhombi are not two shapes — they are one shape seen from two angles:

Generator pair3D corner angleShadow in the plane
|Δj| = 163.4349°thick rhomb (72°)
|Δj| = 2116.5651°thin rhomb (144°)

So whether a tile looks thick or thin depends only on which corner of the golden rhombus lands at the shared vertex.

Four tent poles

All five generators share the same height, z = 1/√5. Every edge of the roof therefore rises or falls by exactly s/√5, inclined arctan(½) = 26.565° to the horizontal — there is no other slope anywhere on the surface.

A vertex's height is index · s/√5, where the index is Σ nj. That index only ever takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4. If you built this as a real roof, you would need exactly four lengths of tent pole:

The whole roof, however far it extends, is trapped between two parallel planes just 3s/√5 ≈ 1.342 s apart. It is a shallow surface — hills and dales, not peaks and canyons.

Fold angles

Every dihedral angle on the surface is one of three values, and every fold is a multiple of 36°:

NeighborsDihedralFold
thick | thick144°36° always
thick | thin144° or 108°36° or 72°
thin | thin72°108° always
There is no flat edge. A coplanar (0°) join looks possible if you enumerate local configurations in the abstract, but it would require two consecutive rhombi in a de Bruijn ribbon to reuse the same second direction. Lines of family j±1 cross a ribbon at spacing 1.051 and family j±2 at 1.701, so each gap of one family always contains a crossing of the other. The second index can never repeat, and every interior edge of a Wieringa roof is a genuine crease.

Mountain and valley divide roughly evenly within each row above, so which way a crease folds is a property of that particular edge — it cannot be read off the pair of tiles. Flipping an entire strip over does swap all of its folds at once, so orientation stays a choice you make at assembly time.

Curvature, and why nets must be cut

Sum the face angles meeting at a vertex and subtract from 360°. What is left is the angular defect: positive means a cone pointing up or down, negative means a saddle.

The negative rows are the saddles, and they are the entire difficulty of net-making: more than 360° of paper wants to meet at one point, so the faces around such a vertex cannot all stay joined. Every saddle forces at least one cut.

Ribbon strips

There is a way to sidestep the problem completely. Follow one de Bruijn line through the tiling and the rhombi it crosses form a ribbon; consecutive rhombi share an edge parallel to the same direction Ej. So every crease in a ribbon is parallel to every other — and a polyhedral surface whose creases are all parallel is a generalized cylinder, which flattens into a straight band with parallel creases spaced at cumulative arc length.

Each face contributes width sin 63.4349° = 2/√5 ≈ 0.894 s, always positive, so crease positions increase monotonically and the band can never fold back on itself:

Ribbon strips cannot self-overlap, at any length. Not "usually" — never, and with no test required.

The site no longer builds nets this way — a ribbon only reaches the two fifths of rhombi sharing its direction, so strips cost about five times the pieces, and the branch-cut routing beats them outright. The theorem stands regardless, and it is the reason a widened ribbon makes such a good backbone.

The sequence of thick and thin along a ribbon is Sturmian in the ratio φ : 1 (crossing densities sin 72° against sin 144°, which is also the source of the tiling's overall φ : 1 tile ratio). Each face shifts the band sideways by ±s/√5, and because Sturmian sequences have bounded discrepancy, a strip stays inside a fixed band around a straight mean axis however long it grows. It veers left and right forever without ever wandering off.

The solids behind it

Three solids underlie all of this — the rhombic triacontahedron and the two golden rhombohedra — and every face of all three is the same golden rhombus. They have their own page, with diagrams.
