Mathematics

Everything the net needs, and why the surface behaves the way it does. Every figure below has been verified numerically against a 349-rhomb patch.

The golden rhombus

One shape does all the work. A golden rhombus is the rhombus whose diagonals stand in the ratio φ : 1 — so the rectangle you get by drawing both diagonals is a golden rectangle. Its acute angle is

θ = arctan 2 = arccos(1/√5) = 63.4349°,   and 116.5651° obtuse.

Why one shape suffices

Take the six five-fold axes of an icosahedron. Put one on the vertical and the other five form a cone around it:

Ej = ( 2/√5 · cos 72j° ,  2/√5 · sin 72j° ,  1/√5 ),   j = 0…4

A Penrose vertex Σ nj ζj in the plane lifts to Σ nj Ej in space. Because Ej · Ek = ±1/√5 for every pair, all ten face orientations are the same golden rhombus. The thick and thin Penrose rhombi are not two shapes — they are one shape seen from two angles:

Generator pair3D corner angleShadow in the plane
|Δj| = 163.4349°thick rhomb (72°)
|Δj| = 2116.5651°thin rhomb (144°)

So whether a tile looks thick or thin depends only on which corner of the golden rhombus lands at the shared vertex.

Four tent poles

All five generators share the same height, z = 1/√5. Every edge of the roof therefore rises or falls by exactly s/√5, inclined arctan(½) = 26.565° to the horizontal — there is no other slope anywhere on the surface.

A vertex's height is index · s/√5, where the index is Σ nj. That index only ever takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4. If you built this as a real roof, you would need exactly four lengths of tent pole:

The whole roof, however far it extends, is trapped between two parallel planes just 3s/√5 ≈ 1.342 s apart. It is a shallow surface — hills and dales, not peaks and canyons.

Fold angles

Every dihedral angle on the surface is one of three values, and every fold is a multiple of 36°:

NeighboursDihedralFold
thick | thick144°36° always
thick | thin144° or 108°36° or 72°
thin | thin72°108° always
There is no flat edge. A coplanar (0°) join looks possible if you enumerate local configurations in the abstract, but it would require two consecutive rhombi in a de Bruijn ribbon to reuse the same second direction. Lines of family j±1 cross a ribbon at spacing 1.051 and family j±2 at 1.701, so each gap of one family always contains a crossing of the other. The second index can never repeat, and every interior edge of a Wieringa roof is a genuine crease.

Mountain and valley divide roughly evenly within each row above, so which way a crease folds is a property of that particular edge — it cannot be read off the pair of tiles. Flipping an entire strip over does swap all of its folds at once, so orientation stays a choice you make at assembly time.

Curvature, and why nets must be cut

Sum the face angles meeting at a vertex and subtract from 360°. What is left is the angular defect: positive means a cone pointing up or down, negative means a saddle.

The negative rows are the saddles, and they are the entire difficulty of net-making: more than 360° of paper wants to meet at one point, so the faces around such a vertex cannot all stay joined. Every saddle forces at least one cut.

Ribbon strips

There is a way to sidestep the problem completely. Follow one de Bruijn line through the tiling and the rhombi it crosses form a ribbon; consecutive rhombi share an edge parallel to the same direction Ej. So every crease in a ribbon is parallel to every other — and a polyhedral surface whose creases are all parallel is a generalized cylinder, which flattens into a straight band with parallel creases spaced at cumulative arc length.

Each face contributes width sin 63.4349° = 2/√5 ≈ 0.894 s, always positive, so crease positions increase monotonically and the band can never fold back on itself:

Ribbon strips cannot self-overlap, at any length. Not "usually" — never, and with no test required. That is why this site builds nets from strips rather than by unfolding outward from a seed tile.

The sequence of thick and thin along a ribbon is Sturmian in the ratio φ : 1 (crossing densities sin 72° against sin 144°, which is also the source of the tiling's overall φ : 1 tile ratio). Each face shifts the band sideways by ±s/√5, and because Sturmian sequences have bounded discrepancy, a strip stays inside a fixed band around a straight mean axis however long it grows. It veers left and right forever without ever wandering off.

The solids behind it

Three solids underlie all of this — the rhombic triacontahedron and the two golden rhombohedra — and every face of all three is the same golden rhombus. They have their own page, with diagrams.
