Packing

The ping-pong balls, on their own terms. Every triacontahedron under the roof has an insphere, all of one radius, and this page is about how those balls sit against each other rather than about what they explain. The roof turns out to be a slice through a contact network larger than itself.

How it works

One radius, and it is not chosen. The rhombic triacontahedron is isohedral, so all thirty of its face planes are tangent to a single insphere, each touching at its own face's centroid. Every ball here is that insphere, so they are all the same size — ρ = √(1 + 2/√5) = 1.3763819 — and each touches the roof at the exact center of every rhomb its solid carries. Centers is where that comes from.

Contacts. Two balls whose centers are exactly 2ρ apart are externally tangent. Measured across every patch, every such contact lies along a face normal, so the polyhedra meet face to face and a ball tangency is always a genuine face tangency — the balls never kiss at a point where the solids would interpenetrate.

Coordination is only ever 0, 2, 3 or 4. Never one, never more than four, on any patch measured. That is far short of the kissing number 12 for equal spheres, so this is a sparse contact network rather than a dense packing — and the balls are free to overlap, which they do: on the Sun at generation 4 there are 4,055 contacts against 4,645 overlapping pairs.

The roof is a slice. A contact whose shared face is an actual rhomb of the roof is what the Centers page calls a shared rhomb, and the two balls kiss at that rhomb's exact center. But those are the minority — 920 of 4,055 on the Sun at generation 4. The rest happen on faces the surface never reaches: the vertical ones a single-valued roof cannot carry, and the ones facing away from it. So the packing is a three-dimensional object of which the roof sees about a quarter. Only balls touching on the roof isolates that quarter.

The network is fragmented. 410 components at Sun generation 4, the largest holding 41% of the balls. Largest component only shows its shape.

Kissing circles. Whether this connects to the classical tangent-circle results is open and worth chasing. Descartes' theorem and the Apollonian gasket need circles of varying radius, and every ball here has the same one, so the curvature relation degenerates rather than applying. The more promising direction is the other way round: slice the packing with a plane and tangent spheres become tangent circles of varying radius. That has not been tried.
