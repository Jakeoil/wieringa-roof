3D Prototype

The roof itself. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom. Every face is the same golden rhombus, and the whole surface is only 1.342 side lengths deep — shallow in numbers, but the hills and dales read unmistakably.

What you are looking at

Colour by star / boat / diamond shows the gen-1 clusters the patch was built from — blue stars, yellow boats, orange diamonds, the same palette as penrose-mosaic. Every rhomb belongs to exactly one, because the expansion always bottoms out at generation 1. It is the clearest way to see the hierarchy survive the lift into three dimensions.

Colour by height shows the four index levels directly — every vertex of the surface sits at index · s/√5 for an index of 1, 2, 3 or 4, and nothing in between. Those four bands are the four tent-pole lengths you would need to hold the roof up over a flat floor.

Colour by thick/thin shows the shadow: the tiles are all congruent golden rhombi in space, and it is only their tilt that makes them project to the wide or narrow Penrose rhomb. Turn the model until you are looking straight down the vertical axis and the surface flattens into the Penrose tiling it came from.

Isoglosses are contour lines — seven per rhombus, dividing its long diagonal into eight. That diagonal is the height gradient, since a rhombus's vertex heights run i, i+1, i+2, i+1 around the cycle; and because a rhombus has perpendicular diagonals, lines across it perpendicular to that axis are exactly level sets of height. They land on quarter-index steps and, since heights agree along every shared edge, they run on unbroken from tile to tile across the whole surface. Drawn flat in 2D they are what makes the corrugation visible; here they are simply the contours they always were.

Transparent drops the surface to half opacity so you can see the folds behind the ones facing you — useful with isoglosses on, since the contours then read through the whole depth of the roof at once.

Vertical scale runs from the true surface at 1 down to nothing at 0, where the roof collapses into the flat Penrose tiling that is its own shadow. Sliding it down is the projection happening in front of you. It only ever flattens — the true shape needs no exaggerating to read, so 1 is the default and there is nothing above it.

Scaling the vertical is an affine map, so faces stay planar all the way down and fold directions stay honest. Below 1 the rhombi are no longer golden, and at 0 they are the thick and thin Penrose rhombs exactly.

Flip reflects the surface in the horizontal plane. That is not a trick of the rendering — it is a second, equally valid Wieringa roof over the same tiling, with every hill turned into a dale and every mountain crease into a valley. The tiling determines the roof only up to this one choice. Fold magnitudes are untouched, so the same cut pieces build either version; you just fold each crease the other way.
