Workbench

Watch branch-cut routing lay a net out step by step — or take over at any point and choose the route yourself.

How it works
What this page is. A Penrose rhomb tiling on the left, and on the right the same rhombi unfolded flat — the net you cut out and fold back into the roof. Every face is the same golden rhombus, so the net is a single shape repeated; what changes is where each one lands and which edges stay attached.

The four lines of controls

Type · Gen · Side sit above the tabs rather than inside a view. The patch is the subject of all three views, so it stays put when you switch, and you can step through seeds and generations while looking at whichever view answers your question. Side belongs there too — it is the rhombus edge length, and it decides how many sheets the net needs, so the Sheets view reports the same field rather than offering a second one that could disagree. At the default 1 in a sheet holds about twenty rhombi, and every generation-2 patch fits one Letter sheet.

Height · Color · isoglosses are how the tiling is drawn, and none of them touch the net. Height is one control doing one job: its sign flips the lift — every hill a dale — and its magnitude is how hard height is shaded, with the middle of the travel leaving the tiles flat. Color switches between the generation-1 clusters, the two mosaic palettes, thick against thin, height index, and the Kowalewski five. Isoglosses are contour lines of constant height, seven per rhombus on quarter-index steps.

Kowalewski five is not a scheme invented for the tiling — it comes down from the rhombic triacontahedron. A rhomb is spanned by two of the five lifting axes, those are five of the six axes of the solid, and the solid's own five-colouring (a proper edge colouring of K6, which is what makes every rosette show all five) hands each rhomb a colour with nothing to seed and nothing to choose. See the Cage for where it comes from. On the tiling it turns out to be a proper colouring too, which it was not asked to be: no two rhombi sharing an edge take the same colour — 0 of 4,715 adjacent pairs on the Sun at generation 3 — the five come out exactly equidistributed, and every Pe5 rosette shows all five, which makes a cup the place to start looking. Height shading is left off in this mode, since two rhombi differing only in shade would read as the same colour.

Play and the transport replay the search that produced the net. Create sheets splits it across paper. Both are described below.

Watching the search

The replay is not a re-enactment. The search emits a step log as it runs and this reads it back, so what you see is the very run that produced the net on screen.

While it runs the tiling shows the search's own state — yellow placed, red tried and rejected for overlap, violet the move being made right now, with its candidate ghosted on the net. Those are colors about the run, so when it finishes they go and the tiling returns to whichever color you chose. The view is fitted once to the finished net and then held, since re-fitting every frame makes the picture lurch.

Play is a traffic light. White waiting, yellow running, green finished. Pressing it while it is yellow queues the split, so a long replay can be left alone and will arrive at sheets by itself; pressing again cancels that. Pause has its own button, because a generation-3 replay is some nine hundred steps and stopping to look at one is the whole reason to watch.

There is only one method now — branch cuts — and no mode to choose first.

Taking over by hand

The canvas is always live. Click a rhomb on the tiling to unfold it onto the net; click an edge instead to say which hinge it should arrive on. Alt-click removes, in either canvas, so the modifier means the same thing wherever you are.

Green means a route lands clean; red means every route from there overlaps. The preview runs each hinge a rhomb could arrive on and tests it before you commit, so the dead ends are visible in advance. Watch the red spread as you work round a saddle vertex — that is the surface's curvature showing up as a constraint on what you are allowed to do next.

Hovering shows the ghosts. A rhomb touching the net on more than one edge has more than one possible landing place, and they can disagree — one hinge clean, another overlapping. Hovering draws all of them; hovering the edge you want narrows it to that one. Hovering either canvas also highlights the twin in the other, with a marker at the corresponding point inside it. The two rhombi are not the same shape — 72° against 63.43° — so the pointer is carried across in the rhombus's own edge coordinates, which tracks corners and edges exactly.

One consequence worth knowing. The scrubber describes the log, not the canvas, so the first edit of your own detaches it: the transport goes away and Play changes to offer running the search again. That would replace what you built, so it asks first.

Since hinges are what hold the net together, taking out an interior rhomb can leave two islands that only look like one net — you will be told when that happens.

On a touch screen a tap places and the buttons all work, but there is no hover and no Alt key, so the ghost previews and Alt-removal are unavailable. Splitting it across paper

Create sheets is the second traffic light. Yellow standing by, green done — and once it is green it stops being a verb and becomes the way through to the Sheets view. It does not throw you there by itself: going green is the answer, and whether you want to look yet is your business. Sheets is the same page, so nothing is re-computed and switching back is instant.

A generation-3 net is several times the size of a sheet, so at that scale this is what makes it buildable rather than merely correct. The split always runs along a hinge, never through a rhombus, so every rhombus lands whole on one sheet. Because the hinges form a spanning tree, pages are always joins + 1: there is no trade-off between fewer sheets and fewer joins, and both are minimized together.

Each severed hinge becomes a taped join carrying a capital letter and the number of the sheet its other half is on — a rhomb reading A▸3 tapes to the rhomb reading A▸1 on sheet 3. Each join is drawn as a tab outside the cut, shaped like the rhombus it joins to: because a rhombus's adjacent angles are supplementary, truncating that neighbor at text height gives a parallelogram, so the tab is literally a slice of the piece that continues elsewhere. The letter runs parallel to the cut.

Every page shares one orientation, so you can lay the printed sheets on a table and see them line up. The best orientation is found by testing the net's own edge directions and their perpendiculars — the minimum enclosing rectangle always has a side flush with a hull edge, and a development only ever uses about nine directions, so this is an exhaustive check of a couple of dozen angles rather than a search. Every sheet also carries a thumbnail of the whole net with that sheet shaded and every join marked, placed in whichever corner the net leaves clearest.

Once a net is split, the tiling canvas here shows the partition, each region in its sheet's color: the same picture you built on, now divided. Changing the patch discards the sheets, since they described a net that no longer exists.

Reading the net

Creases are dashed by fold angle — 36°, 72°, 108° — and colored red for mountain, blue for valley. Solid black edges are cuts. Overlapping placements are still allowed: they go down faded with a red dashed outline.

Shading is structural, not decorative, so it agrees with the creases: a tile is lighter the higher it sits, which puts the red mountains along the light ridges and the blue valleys down the dark troughs. The ramp is absolute across the whole patch — a rhombus rising 1→3 prints lighter than one rising 2→4 — so the shading tells you how high a tile is, not merely which way it tilts.

The net panel is not to scale on screen — it is a faithful scale model of the sheet, so the rhombi are the right size relative to the sheet outline drawn around them, but how big that is depends on your display. Printing is true size. A net too big for the printable frame is still drawn, spilling past it, with the frame turning pink and telling you how far over: snip along it.

Print net is the overview: the whole net on one page, shrunk however far it takes to fit. Print sheets / PDF on the Sheets view outputs the lot, and Save as PDF gives one file — both as vector at true size, through the renderer in sheet.ts.

Layer appears only when a net needs more than one sheet. Where the surface wraps over itself it need not be cut — the offending part continues on the next layer, which is the next sheet of paper, and every layer is flat by construction. Choosing one draws it solid and ghosts the rest. All layers share one coordinate frame, so the printed sheets register against each other and the surface visibly continues across the seam. Up to generation 3 every patch is a single layer and the control stays hidden.

The seeds

All nine are here: the three Pe pentagons, the St star, boat and diamond, and the three composites Queen, Sun and Star. The patch controls stay live while watching, so you can switch seed or generation mid-replay; doing so starts the search again. Changing the patch on Penta vs Rhombs does not run a search, since there is no net on screen there to route.

Rhombs — the tiles they produce

The pentagons are Penrose's P1 tiling and the rhombs are P3; every rhomb belongs to exactly one penta tile. The tiles that emit no rhombs — stars, boats and diamonds — are the gaps, and they are invisible on the right precisely because they produce nothing. They are drawn on the left dashed, because those are the places a composite can be extended.

Scale is not asserted, it is derived: a pentagon's circumradius equals the rhomb edge exactly, which puts adjacent pentagons two apothems apart, edge to edge. Checked across every seed — no P1 tile overlaps another.
Side 1 in — set on the Workbench
Joins. Each cut carries a tab shaped like the rhombus it joins to — the adjoining rhombus truncated at text height, a parallelogram because a rhombus's adjacent angles are supplementary. It is lettered with the sheet its other half is on: A▸3 tapes to A▸1 on sheet 3.

Every sheet carries a mini of the patch with its own rhombi filled in the sheet's color and outlined along its cuts. The same color keys this list, the mini, the Map, and the tiling canvas on the Workbench — so a sheet and its region read as one thing.

All sheets share one orientation, so the printed pages lie together the way they assemble. Print at 100% — no scaling, no fit-to-page — and measure a rhombus edge against the side length before cutting. Back side draws the underside, which turns the whole reading over: hills become dales and mountains become valleys, since a ridge seen from below is a trough. Shading and creases move together, always. Reflection does not matter, since every patch here has a mirror axis.
