Exceptional Singularity

The one point in the Penrose plane where the roof stands up. A decagon needs every phase integral, and since only the fractional parts matter that is the single phase vector that is its own class — so this is not a rare configuration, it is one point, and no other Penrose class has one anywhere. Phase 1: the patch it lives in, as a Wieringa roof.

How it works

Why one point. A decagon in the pentagrid needs every phase γⱼ integral, and since only γⱼ mod 1 matters that is γ ≡ 0 — the one phase vector that is its own class. The class invariant Σ(γⱼ mod 1) is 0 for that vector alone and 1 or 2 for every other Penrose patch, so no other class has a decagon anywhere. It is not rare; it is a single point in the whole plane, and that is what makes it exceptional.

What a singularity is. Where k grid lines meet, the 2k regions around the meeting are the corners of a k-cube, and the plane had only ever shown its shadow. Lift each region to its own height and that cube embeds in the roof's three-space as a zonohedron — not a picture of one, the solid itself. Three lines give a golden rhombohedron, four Bilinski's dodecahedron, five the golden rhombic icosahedron F₂₀, which is the same solid the Cage builds from five of the six icosahedral axes. Every edge is √5/2 and every face has diagonal ratio φ: the roof's own golden rhombus. A singularity is not a defect in the roof. It is a piece of the roof's solid geometry that the plane was flattening.

This page, so far. Phase 1 draws the patch and nothing else: the Queen composite at generation 3, which is the cartwheel, and the cartwheel is this singularity. Every rhombus is the same golden rhombus at a different tilt and orientation — there is no thick and no thin here, only the one shape seen from different angles, which is what a Wieringa roof is. The decagon at the center is already one of the ten configurations it can take.

Five generators, not six. The roof lifts on five of the six icosahedral five-fold axes. The sixth is the vertical, and a pentagrid can never supply it: a zone is a family of parallel lines, and that one would need plane normal 0 — a family whose lines have no direction. Adding it is the step from Penrose's 5→2 to the icosahedral 6→3. So the coloring here runs on five axes, and the Kowalewski five is exactly five: χ′(K₅) = 5, the least K₅ admits.

Bands colors by ribbon instead. The faces sharing an edge direction form a strip, and every face lies in exactly two — so five ribbon families and ten kinds of crossing. A face shows both of its colors as bands rather than mixed, because the ten pairwise blends of five saturated hues come no closer than ΔE 17 to one another where the five themselves sit 42 apart. Ribbon sets their width and clear ground drops everything outside them, leaving the roof as its ribbons alone.
