Exceptional Singularity

The one point in the Penrose plane where the roof stands up. A decagon needs every phase integral, and since only the fractional parts matter that is the single phase vector that is its own class — so this is not a rare configuration, it is one point, and no other Penrose class has one anywhere. Phase 1: the patch it lives in, as a Wieringa roof.

How it works

One point. A decagon needs every grid phase integral, and only the fractional parts matter, so that is the single phase vector that is its own class. No other Penrose class has one anywhere. This is the patch it lives in — the Deca composite at generation 3, the cartwheel — drawn as a Wieringa roof. Every rhombus is the same golden rhombus at a different tilt.

A singularity is a solid. Where k grid lines meet, the 2k regions around them are the corners of a k-cube; lift each to its own height and that cube becomes a zonohedron in the roof's own space. Three lines give a rhombohedron — a skewb, A₆ or O₆ — and five give F₂₀, the same solid the Cage builds from five of the six icosahedral axes. F₂₀ is drawn with its two poles removed, which is what makes it the singularity rather than the solid.

Click one. Superposed it is the solid, all its readings at once; click and it collapses to one — ten for F₂₀, two for a skewb — and clicking on advances, then returns to superposition. F₂₀'s ten factor as a 5-state rotation times a 2-state flip, the flip being the roof turned over.

A vertex star is simply the set of rhombi meeting at one corner, named by the angles they bring: five thick rhombi round a point is 72+72+72+72+72, the sun. Sum to 360° and the corner closes. Only seven stars ever occur in a Penrose tiling — measured here from 10,639 real ones — and two of them are our own figures: 108+108+144 is the oblate skewb and 72+144+144 the acute. Others add to 360° perfectly well and still never appear; 36+108+108+108 is one.

That is the whole cascade. A collapse leaves the figure's rim alone, so nothing travels through the boundary — what it changes is the star at each corner it touches. So after a collapse every figure is asked how many of its readings still leave every star among the seven, and any down to one collapses too. No phases, no grid, only the arrows. Release puts everything back.

Why ten. The decagon has 62 rhombic tilings. Requiring every vertex index in 1…4 — no path through an extreme — leaves 40. Requiring legal stars leaves 10, and a sweep of 760 singular decagons across thirty patches finds that same ten every time and the other thirty never.
