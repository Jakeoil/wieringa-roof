Cage

The triacontahedron opened up. It falls into exactly twenty golden rhombic hexahedra — ten acute and ten obtuse — and this is them, as a cage you can turn and pull apart. Chapter 4 begins here.

How it works

Twenty cells, and the count is forced. A zonohedron on n generators falls into C(n,3) parallelepipeds, one for each triple of generators — the cell those three span. The triacontahedron is the zonohedron on the six icosahedral five-fold axes, so C(6,3) = 20, and because every pairwise dot product of those axes is ±1/√5 every cell comes out a golden rhombic hexahedron. Ten are obtuse with volume 0.470228 and ten acute with 0.760845, in the ratio φ, and they sum to 4√(5+2√5) = 12.310734 — the triacontahedron's own volume, exactly.

So the classical "ten of each" is not an arrangement anybody had to find. The count comes from the triples and the shapes come from the axes.

The dissection is a choice; the cells are not. A point of the solid is ½Σcₘaₘ with every cₘ in [−1,1], and a cell fixes cₘ = ±1 on three axes while letting the other three run. A cell is therefore a triple plus a sign vector, and a dissection is one sign choice per triple with no two cells overlapping. Many dissections exist. This page shows one, found by search rather than asserted, and checked: no two cells overlap, and 93,809 sampled interior points each lie in exactly one cell.

Exploding moves each cell outward along the direction of its own center, so at zero they reassemble exactly and at full travel they hold the same arrangement, magnified. Nothing turns and nothing is reordered — the picture only spreads. Faces go translucent while the cells are still close, because assembled they share every internal face and opaque ones would hide the whole dissection behind the solid's own surface.

There are exactly two dissections, up to rotation and reflection, which is why the choice is a toggle. They differ by a single flip inside one Bilinski dodecahedron — the zonohedron on four of the six axes, which admits exactly two tilings of its own. Four cells and six internal faces change; the other sixteen cells, and the whole shell, are identical. That elementary move is the phason flip of the quasicrystal literature. With positions fixed in space there are 160, and they fall into two orbits under the solid's symmetry group of order 120 — one of size 120 and one of size 40. The large orbit keeps no symmetry whatever; the small one keeps a three-fold axis. Both are ten acute and ten obtuse, as they must be.

That is worth knowing when looking at the inner cage. The solid's own thirty faces carry all 60 of its rotations; the forty-five internal faces of the chiral dissection carry exactly one, the identity. The exterior is forced and the interior is chosen, and one of the two available choices throws the symmetry away entirely.

The Kowalewski five. There is a five-colouring of the triacontahedron's thirty faces in which every rosette shows all five colours, and it is forced rather than designed. A face is spanned by a pair of the six axes, and the five faces round a five-valent vertex are exactly the five pairs containing that vertex's axis — so "all five in every rosette" says that pairs sharing an axis must differ, which is a proper edge colouring of K₆. K₆ needs exactly five colours, and a colour class is a perfect matching of three pairs.

Three things Jeff described then follow without being asked for. Opposite faces of the solid share an axis pair, so they share a colour. A hexahedron's six faces come in three opposite pairs, one per axis pair of its triple, so opposite faces of each hexahedron are the same colour automatically. And the three pairs of a triple share an axis pairwise, so every hexahedron wears exactly three of the five — with C(5,3) = 10 such triples, each borne once by an acute cell and once by an obtuse one. That is the labelling of the classical Kowalewski puzzle, whose colour-matching assemblies George Hart counts at 320. All of it is checked in tools/dissect.mjs.
