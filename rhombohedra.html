Cage

The triacontahedron opened up. It falls into exactly twenty golden rhombic hexahedra — ten acute and ten obtuse — and this is them, as a cage you can turn and pull apart. Drop an axis, or two, and the same cage holds the golden icosahedron and the golden dodecahedron. Chapter 4 begins here.

How it works

Twenty cells, and the count is forced. A zonohedron on n generators falls into C(n,3) parallelepipeds, one for each triple of generators — the cell those three span. The triacontahedron is the zonohedron on the six icosahedral five-fold axes, so C(6,3) = 20, and because every pairwise dot product of those axes is ±1/√5 every cell comes out a golden rhombic hexahedron. Ten are obtuse with volume 0.470228 and ten acute with 0.760845, in the ratio φ, and they sum to 4√(5+2√5) = 12.310734 — the triacontahedron's own volume, exactly.

So the classical "ten of each" is not an arrangement anybody had to find. The count comes from the triples and the shapes come from the axes.

And nothing in that says six. Keep five of the axes and the zonohedron on them is the golden icosahedron: C(5,2)·2 = 20 faces and C(5,3) = 10 cells, five acute and five obtuse. Keep four and it is the golden dodecahedron — Bilinski's — twelve faces and four cells, two of each. Because all fifteen pairs of the six axes meet at the same angle, |cos| = 1/√5, every face of all three is the same golden rhombus and every cell one of the same two hexahedra: the solids differ in how many pieces, never in which. Their volumes are 2.462147, 6.155367 and 12.310734 — the ratio 2 : 5 : 10. Which four or five axes you keep makes no difference either, all fifteen 4-subsets being congruent and all six 5-subsets too.

Keep three and the zonohedron is a single cell — and there the choice of triple is the whole point. Ten of the twenty triples span the acute hexahedron, volume 0.760845, and ten the oblate one, 0.470228, in the ratio φ. Both are on the menu because they are the two shapes every larger solid in the family is built from: the triacontahedron is ten of each, the icosahedron five of each, the dodecahedron two. Neither explodes — with one cell the center is the origin and there is nowhere to travel — and neither has a cage within, its six faces all being on the surface.

The dissection is a choice; the cells are not. A point of the solid is ½Σcₘaₘ with every cₘ in [−1,1], and a cell fixes cₘ = ±1 on three axes while letting the other three run. A cell is therefore a triple plus a sign vector, and a dissection is one sign choice per triple with no two cells overlapping. Many dissections exist. This page shows one, found by search rather than asserted, and checked: no two cells overlap, and 93,809 sampled interior points each lie in exactly one cell.

Exploding moves each cell outward along the direction of its own center, so at zero they reassemble exactly and at full travel they hold the same arrangement, magnified. Nothing turns and nothing is reordered — the picture only spreads. Faces go translucent while the cells are still close, because assembled they share every internal face and opaque ones would hide the whole dissection behind the solid's own surface.

Orthogonal drops the perspective. Parallel edges come out parallel, and two cells of the same kind come out the same size wherever they sit — which is worth having here, because the cells are parallelepipeds and the whole dissection is a statement about parallel directions. The cost is the depth cue: with no convergence to read, near and far stop announcing themselves, so it helps to be turning. Ticking it changes the projection and nothing else — the frustum is sized to the apparent height the perspective camera had, so the solid does not jump.

Eye light decides where the lamps stand. Left in the scene they stay still in the world while the camera walks round, so the lit side of the solid stays put and slides across the screen — which reads as a viewer circling a statue. Carried with the viewpoint they travel with the eye, so the lit side stays where you are and the faces brighten and dim as they turn through it — which reads as the solid turning. Neither is more correct; they are different questions about what is moving.

It earns its keep on the hexahedra. Six faces of one rhombus, no feature to tell any of them apart: this is the Necker cube's own ambiguity, and a lone rhombohedron lit from off in the world can be read inside out. Carried with the eye, the shading says which way is which. The solids are also framed to their own size rather than all to the triacontahedron's — circumradii run 1.6180, 1.4644, 1.3764 and 0.9867, so a fixed frame left a hexahedron small and distant, which is precisely where perspective had least to say. Fitting brings it from 6.18 back to 3.77 and the foreshortening with it. The angle you are looking from is never touched, only the distance, and only when the solid changes.

The triacontahedron has exactly two dissections, up to rotation and reflection, which is why the choice is a toggle — and it alone has a choice. They differ by a single flip inside one Bilinski dodecahedron — the zonohedron on four of the six axes, which admits exactly two tilings of its own. Four cells and six internal faces change; the other sixteen cells, and the whole shell, are identical. That elementary move is the phason flip of the quasicrystal literature. With positions fixed in space there are 160, and they fall into two orbits under the solid's symmetry group of order 120 — one of size 120 and one of size 40. The large orbit keeps no symmetry whatever; the small one keeps a three-fold axis. Both are ten acute and ten obtuse, as they must be.

The smaller solids have no choice at all. Each has to be reduced under its own symmetries — those carrying its axis set to itself, which is all 120 at six axes but only 20 at five and 8 at four. Do that and the golden icosahedron's ten labelled dissections come to a single orbit, 10 = 20/2, and the dodecahedron's two to one, 2 = 8/4. Nothing is left over, so the flip control goes quiet on both. It also says what the dodecahedron's pair really is: one dissection twice placed, a rotation of the dodecahedron carrying either onto the other. What that rotation is not is a symmetry of the triacontahedron around it — which is exactly why turning that one dodecahedron over changes the larger dissection while leaving the shell untouched.

That is worth knowing when looking at the inner cage. The solid's own thirty faces carry all 60 of its rotations; the forty-five internal faces of the chiral dissection carry exactly one, the identity. The exterior is forced and the interior is chosen, and one of the two available choices throws the symmetry away entirely.

Singularity deletes the poles. Of the golden icosahedron's twenty-two vertices, exactly two have all five zones running through them — the extreme pair along the five-fold axis, which is the sixth axis the solid left out. The other twenty run ten of valence four and ten of valence three. Nowhere else in the family has such a pair: the triacontahedron has no vertex on six faces at all, and below five axes valence-k vertices come in quantity rather than in pairs — every corner of a parallelepiped is one — so the option is offered on this solid alone.

Take each pole away and everything carrying it goes: five faces and five edges, and since the pole belongs to three cells — two oblate and one acute — three of the six faces of each. Eighteen of the sixty cell faces in all, from six of the ten cells; the other four never reach a pole. What is left at each end is a rim of ten edges on ten vertices, a skew decagon alternating between the four-valent vertices and the three-valent ones. The edges fall out rather than being arranged: an edge is drawn as part of a face's boundary, and every edge at a pole lies only in faces at that pole. The cage is left whole, so the holes are visible against the sockets the cells came out of.

And that is not the end of it. With the poles gone, two vertices at each end are left carrying only two edges — one ring down the five-fold axis, at height ±0.670820 — and a vertex with two edges is no longer a corner of anything. The single face still holding it is joined to the rest of the surface along one edge at each side and nothing else, hanging off the rim rather than forming part of it. So it goes too: four more faces, two at each end, each the outer face of one of the four oblate cells that reach a pole. Twenty-two of the sixty cell faces in all. One round settles it — the lowest degree left is three — which is a result rather than an assumption, the rule being to delete what is left hanging and keep deleting. Degrees are counted on distinct faces, since an internal face belongs to two cells and its edges are one edge, not two; which is also what the eye counts.

The Kowalewski five. There is a five-coloring of the triacontahedron's thirty faces in which every rosette shows all five colors, and it is forced rather than designed. A face is spanned by a pair of the six axes, and the five faces round a five-valent vertex are exactly the five pairs containing that vertex's axis — so "all five in every rosette" says that pairs sharing an axis must differ, which is a proper edge coloring of K₆. K₆ needs exactly five colors, and a color class is a perfect matching of three pairs.

Bands colors by ribbon rather than by class. The faces sharing an edge direction form a strip — the de Bruijn ribbon, the worm of the pentagrid — and since every face is spanned by two axes, every face lies in exactly two ribbons. So there are k bands and C(k,2) kinds of face: five and ten on the golden icosahedron, which is the pentagrid's own count and where the scheme is most at home. The five colors are the Kowalewski five doing a different job, because on five axes the ribbons are the five line families. The triacontahedron needs a sixth, which is a teal placed ΔE 46 from the nearest of the five, the five sitting 41.6 from each other.

The colors go on the edges — and on the sticks, in ball and stick, where a ribbon is a run of rods all one color. A face then has to carry two, and it carries them as bands rather than as a mixture. The ribbon of an axis joins the two edges that run along that axis, which sit on opposite sides of the rhombus; cut it to width and do the same for the other axis, and the face falls into nine patches: two for each band, one where they cross, and four corners in neither ribbon. Those four are the ground, painted the page's own background so the ribbons read as stripes laid on a tile — and a ribbon runs on across the solid unbroken, which is the whole point of the picture.

Clear ground takes the ground away, and takes it away rather than making it see-through: those patches are not drawn at all, so there is no surface there to be transparent. The solid is then the ribbons and nothing else, with its own interior showing through the gaps — the ribbons are double-sided, so you see their backs through the holes.

Ribbon sets how wide they run, as a fraction of the face. At 50% it is the plaid. Widen it and the gaps close, until at 100% each ribbon covers its whole rhombus, the two coincide and every face is a single crossing. Narrow it and the ribbons thin to threads and then to nothing, leaving only the edges. The drawn fraction of each face is exactly 2w − w² — two ribbons of width w less the square where they overlap — and the ground makes up the rest, so a face is always covered exactly once. Checked rather than asserted, along with the patches tiling each face and each band reaching both of its own edges.

Mixing was tried and measured. The ten pairwise blends of five saturated hues come no closer than ΔE 17 to one another — the best of twenty-four rules, blending in sRGB, in linear light and in Lab at six weights apiece — against 41.6 for the five themselves, and anything under about fifteen reads as the same color. Two of the ten would have been indistinguishable under the shading gradient. Where the bands cross, though, both really are present, and there the mixture is the honest answer: it is the one place in the scheme that a blend says something true.

Five is this solid's number, not the family's. On k axes the demand is the same and the graph is smaller: a proper edge coloring of Kk, whose chromatic index is k−1 for even k and k for odd. So it is five at six axes, five again at five, and only three at four — and three at three. Restricting the six-axis coloring is already the best possible at five axes and at three: at five each perfect matching loses the one pair through the dropped axis and keeps the other two, so no class empties; at three, two pairs of a triple always share an axis and so can never share a class. Four axes is the one exception. All fifteen 4-subsets inherit colors from all five classes where three would do, so the golden dodecahedron is given K₄'s own coloring instead — its three perfect matchings, {01,23}, {02,13}, {03,12}. Everything the five gave up survives it: faces sharing an axis still differ, opposite faces of a hexahedron still agree, and a hexahedron still wears three distinct colors — that last for free, since its three axis pairs share an axis pairwise.

Three things Jake described then follow without being asked for. Opposite faces of the solid share an axis pair, so they share a color. A hexahedron's six faces come in three opposite pairs, one per axis pair of its triple, so opposite faces of each hexahedron are the same color automatically. And the three pairs of a triple share an axis pairwise, so every hexahedron wears exactly three of the five — with C(5,3) = 10 such triples, each borne once by an acute cell and once by an obtuse one. That is the labelling of the classical Kowalewski puzzle, whose color-matching assemblies George Hart counts at 320. All of it is checked in tools/dissect.mjs.
