Tools & Templates

What to cut with, what to fold against, and what to check the folds against — plus true-size templates you can print, or send to a laser cutter in metal or acrylic and keep forever.

One shape, ten orientations

The whole model is a single cut shape. Every face of the Wieringa roof is the same golden rhombus — thick and thin tiles differ only in which corner meets the shared vertex, not in shape. So one template cuts every piece, and one gauge checks every fold.

The angles are not round numbers and never will be — they are arctans of the golden ratio. Do not try to set them on a protractor by eye. Cut one accurate template and trace it, or have one cut for you; every error you build into the template gets repeated a few hundred times.

Golden rhombus at 1 in side — the same size as every generation-2 net on the Sheets page, so a template cut from this fits those nets directly. Both corner angles are repeated as open wedges for setting a bevel or checking a cut. Print at 100%, no scaling, then measure the 2 in bar before trusting anything else on the page.

Fold angles are not the angles you measure

This one is worth reading twice, because getting it wrong means a gauge cut to the wrong shape. The nets label each crease 36, 72 or 108. Those are fold angles — how far the paper turns away from flat. The angle you can actually put a gauge against, between the two panels, is the dihedral, and it is the supplement:

printed on the creaseturn from flatdihedral between panelsfeel
3636°144°gentle
7272°108°firm
108108°72°sharp — the fiddly one

A useful check on all of this: 144° is exactly the dihedral angle of the rhombic triacontahedron, thirty golden rhombi closed into a solid. The roof is locally the same surface, so its gentlest crease matching the RT is the right answer falling out.

Cut the gauge to 144°, 108° and 72° — not 36, 72, 108. The numbers 72 and 108 appear in both columns with different meanings, which is exactly the sort of thing that survives right up until the metal comes back wrong.

Three dihedral gauges. Set the folded pair of faces into the notch; when both panels sit flush against the two arms, the crease is right. Cut these from something rigid — card will splay and tell you what you want to hear.

A folding jig

The gauges above check a fold after the fact. A jig forms it, which is better — a crease set against a rigid edge comes out right the first time instead of being coaxed towards right. And the two shapes are complements: a gauge is the notch, a jig is the block that fits it.

Make three blocks whose apex angle is the target dihedral. Lay the scored sheet over the apex with the score on the ridge, press both halves down onto the two faces, and the paper leaves the block at exactly that angle. Cross-sections, for a 40 mm-ish arm:

Forming blocks, cross-section, drawn true size at these dimensions. The red line is the paper draping over the apex. Extrude each to 100 mm or so along the crease direction — a hardwood offcut planed to the angle, or three strips of acrylic stacked and sanded, or 3D printed.

The one jig that pays for itself: a scoring comb. Inside any de Bruijn ribbon every crease is parallel, and the spacing is exactly 2/√5 · s = 0.894427 s — 0.894 in (22.72 mm) at a 1 in side. So a comb of parallel ridges at that single pitch pre-scores a whole band in one pass, with no measuring and no cumulative drift. This is the useful residue of the ribbon-strip idea: strips were retired as a way to build nets, but the parallel-crease fact that made them work is exactly what makes this jig possible.

Scoring and folding

Do not fold paper freehand and do not cut the creases. Score them: run a bone folder or a dead ballpoint along the line hard enough to crush the fibres, not to cut through. The paper then folds exactly where you put the score and nowhere else, which is the entire difference between a crisp model and a soft one.

Score on the outside of the fold — the face that ends up convex. A mountain crease is scored from the front, a valley from the back, so it helps to sort the sheet into mountains and valleys before you start rather than flipping back and forth. The nets color them for you: red for mountain, blue for valley. Turning a whole piece over swaps them, so if a piece folds more easily the other way up, that is fine and changes nothing.

Work the gentle 36 creases last. They are shallow enough that a sharp neighboring fold will pull them out of true if they are already set.

Paper

100–120 gsm is the sweet spot: heavy enough to hold a dihedral without sagging, light enough that a 72° crease does not crack along the score. Ordinary 80 gsm office paper will fold but will not hold its shape once the model is large. Much above 160 gsm and you need to score both sides.

On size: below about 12 mm side the sharp creases get genuinely fiddly — a 72° dihedral on a 12 mm panel leaves very little to grip. 1 in is the standard side on the net pages, comfortably clear of that, and it also puts about twenty rhombi on a Letter sheet. The side is a free parameter everywhere; 1 in is only the default.

Ordering a laser-cut template

Worth doing if you are going to build more than one model. A cut template in metal or acrylic lasts forever, cannot be trimmed away by your own knife, and removes the compounding error of tracing a paper template with a paper template.

  • What to have cutThe rhombus as a window rather than a solid tile — you want to trace the inside of an opening, not around the outside of a shape, so the knife has something to run against. Plus the three gauge notches, 144° / 108° / 72°.
  • Jigs, from sheetA laser cuts flat stock, so make each forming block as several identical profiles stacked and glued — cut six copies of the 144° triangle in 3 mm acrylic and you have an 18 mm block. Same for a scoring comb: one profile with teeth at 22.72 mm pitch, stacked.
  • Material3 mm acrylic is cheap and accurate and enough for tracing. If you want to cut against it with a blade, use metal — 1.5 mm aluminium, brass or steel — because a blade will shave acrylic and your template will drift.
  • KerfThe beam removes material as it goes, so the cut lands beside the line, not on it. Tell the shop the finished dimensions matter and let them compensate; do not assume a figure yourself, because it varies with machine, material and thickness.
  • What to sendGive them the numbers, not a screenshot: side 1 in (25.400 mm), angles 63.43495° and 116.56505°, or equivalently a rhombus with diagonals 43.213 mm × 26.707 mm. Diagonals are the safer specification — they are lengths, so nothing depends on the shop's angular precision.
Specify the rhombus by its diagonals if you can — 34.026 mm by 43.213 mm by 26.707 mm at a 1 in side. Two perpendicular lengths are unambiguous and easy to inspect with callipers when it arrives. An angle spec has to be trusted.

A second option, if the shop will do it: have the paper laser-scored directly. A low-power vector pass creases without cutting through, which is faster and more accurate than scoring several hundred edges by hand. Send the cut lines and the score lines as separate layers or colors and say clearly which is which.

The kit

  • Bone folderThe one tool that is not optional. Scores and then sets the crease. A cheap plastic one is fine; a Teflon one will not mark the paper.
  • Craft knifeX-Acto or similar with #11 blades. Change the blade far more often than feels reasonable — a dull blade tears rather than cuts, and on a 1 in rhombus the tear is a visible fraction of the edge.
  • Steel ruleNot plastic, not aluminium. A blade will bite into soft edges and follow the notch it made. Cork-backed stops it sliding.
  • Self-healing matKeeps the blade sharp and stops the cut wandering into a groove left by the last one.
  • Embossing stylusOptional alternative for scoring — a ball tip gets closer to a corner than a bone folder can. A ballpoint with no ink left does the same job.
  • Tape, for joiningThin matte tape, 12 mm or narrower. Matte because gloss reflects and shows every join; narrow because the panels are small. Tape on the inside wherever you can.
  • Tape, for holdingWashi or artist's tape. Low tack and repositionable, so you can hold a dihedral while you check it against the gauge and then take it off without lifting the surface.
  • TweezersFor the last few faces, where the model has closed up enough that fingers no longer reach inside.
  • Small clipsBinder clips or clothes pegs, to hold a seam while glue grabs. Pad the jaws or they will emboss the paper.
  • GluePVA, applied sparingly with a cocktail stick, if you would rather have tabs than tape. It is stronger and invisible but slow, and it will cockle thin paper.
  • Cardstock100–120 gsm. Buy more than you think — the first sheet or two are always calibration.

Order of work

Print, then score every crease before cutting anything out. The sheet is far easier to hold flat and register against a rule while it is still a sheet. Cut the outline last. Once a piece is free it will move under the blade, and once it is folded you cannot score it at all.
